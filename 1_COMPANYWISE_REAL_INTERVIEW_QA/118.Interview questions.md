### Q1. How many team members do similar work in your team?
**A:** I work with a cross-functional team that includes developers, QA, and infrastructure teams. There are around **6 members** doing similar work.

### Q2. How does your reporting structure work?
**A:** We report directly to the team lead, and there is also a reporting manager.

### Q3. Can you walk me through a recent incident or request that you handled, where you made a significant contribution?
**A:** I remember an incident involving a production deployment where most of the application pods started failing, and users began receiving errors.

I first checked the deployment timeline, Kubernetes rollout status, pod events, and application logs. I found that the new release had a configuration issue that caused the containers to restart and fail.

I immediately informed the team lead, stopped the rollout, and used the approved rollback process through our environment workflow. I then verified the readiness and endpoints of the services, the health of the ALB, error rates, and application functionality.

Once the service was recovered, I worked with the development team to identify the configuration problem and helped validate the corrected release in a lower environment.

After the incident, I documented the RCA (Root Cause Analysis) and added a validation step to the deployment process so that the same configuration issue would be detected before production.

The team appreciated that I focused first on restoring the service, communicated clearly during the incident, and helped prevent similar issues in the future.

### Q4. How is your work divided between incidents, requests, changes, and improvements?
**A:** I would estimate my work as approximately 30% incidents and production support, and 40% planned changes and deployments, with the remaining work involving automation and improvement.

### Q5. Do you participate in incident bridge calls?
**A:** Yes, I participate in incident bridge calls, especially for production or high-priority incidents. I join quickly, confirm customer impact, and share what I find from Kubernetes logs and monitoring dashboards.

### Q6. Do you execute changes? What types of changes do you handle, and what ground rules do you follow while implementing them?
**A:** I execute planned changes such as Kubernetes deployments, Helm value updates, Jenkins pipeline changes, Terraform infrastructure updates, and configuration changes.

The number varies depending on the release schedule, but I usually handle several changes each week across development, staging, and production environments.

We follow a controlled process. I create or review a change request with the scope, implementation steps, validation checks, and rollback steps. Then I test the change in a lower environment, raise a PR, and get a peer review.

I run validations such as Terraform plan, pipeline checks, scans, and deployment checks. Then I schedule production changes during an approved maintenance window.

### Q7. If you are 90% done with a change but only have two minutes remaining in the maintenance window, would you continue or consider rollback?
**A:** My approach would be that I would not continue just because the change is almost complete. I would follow the approved change plan and the defined maintenance window. If the remaining step is risky, I would not proceed just to complete it within the remaining time.

### Q8. What attributes should an employee have for career growth?
**A:** I think staying relevant in an organization is important. You should not get too attached to one technology, product, or solution. Instead, stay updated with what is happening in the industry and align your career with that.

You cannot become an expert in everything, but you can choose an area such as Kubernetes and build your expertise in it.

Earlier in my career, one person had to handle everything in IT. There were no separate server, network, or enterprise application teams. IT had end-to-end ownership, which gave us opportunities to develop new skills and stay relevant.

Awareness of what is happening around you and what the organization requires will help you progress in your career.

You should also understand what other teams are doing and align that knowledge with your career goals. Learning should never stop.

You need to be strong in something first, and that becomes a platform to explore other opportunities. So, staying relevant, aligning your goals with the organization's goals, and carrying a positive attitude are important.
